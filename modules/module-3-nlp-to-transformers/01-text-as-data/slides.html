The problemText is not numbers

Every model in Module 2 (ML and DL essentials) ate numbers: vectors in, a prediction out. Text is symbols. So the first job is a faithful way to turn words into numbers.

Two stepsFirst split the text into tokens, then map each token to a vector. Get this right and the rest of NLP is the machinery from Module 2 (ML and DL essentials), at scale.

TokensModels read pieces of words

A token is a chunk of text: often a whole common word, sometimes a piece of a rarer one.

Think of it asLego. Common shapes are a single brick; unusual shapes are built from smaller bricks. A tokenizer keeps frequent words whole and breaks rare ones into reusable pieces.

TokensTwo tokenizers, two ways to mark the pieces

Run "I love tokenization!" through two tokenizers. Both break the rare word into pieces, but they flag those pieces differently.

Same idea, two conventionsWordPiece flags a piece that continues the previous token with ##; BPE flags a piece that starts a new word with a leading-space symbol, shown here as ▁ (GPT-2 writes the same thing as Ġ). Either way the output is a list of integer ids, and the two tokenizers can split the same word differently. Keeping the space makes the split reversible: glue the tokens back and you recover the exact text.

FoundationsCorpus and vocabulary

Two words you will hear constantly in NLP:

  • Corpus: the whole body of text a model learns from, such as books, web pages, and code.
  • Vocabulary: the fixed set of tokens the tokenizer knows, each with an id. It is built once from the corpus.
Why it mattersEverything downstream is sized by the vocabulary: a one-hot vector is as long as the vocabulary, and the embedding table has exactly one row per vocabulary token.

Why it worksFrequent patterns earn their own token, so common words end up whole. Anything unseen still tokenizes into smaller known pieces, so no word is ever out of vocabulary. A novel "lowest" would split into lo, w, est.
No cutoff frequencyEach step merges the single most frequent pair, whatever its count. There is no threshold. You repeat this thousands of times, the three above are just the start, and stop once the vocabulary reaches a target size you pick (often 30k to 50k).

The ruleApply the merges in learned (priority) order, not by scanning for the longest piece. A character with no matching rule, here s and w, simply stays its own token. Characters are the floor, so a word never seen in training still tokenizes and is never out of vocabulary.
Resultslowest becomes s · lo · w · est, four tokens. There is no low token because no rule ever creates one; the tokenizer can only build the pieces its merges define.

PreprocessingThe practical bits

Normalize
  • Lowercasing (sometimes), consistent whitespace and punctuation.
  • Keeps the vocabulary from splitting on trivia.
Special tokens
  • Start, end, and padding markers.
  • They tell the model where a sequence begins, ends, and where filler sits.
Padding
  • Batches need equal length (a callback to batching in class 2.5, Training in practice).
  • Short sequences are padded to match the longest.
The pointThese steps make text uniform enough to batch and feed to a model, without changing what the text means.

Tokens to numbersThe naive first attempt, and why it fails

The obvious way to turn a token id into a vector is one-hot: a vector as long as the whole vocabulary, all zeros except a single 1. It works, but it is a poor representation.

  • Huge and sparse: a 50,000-long vector to name one token.
  • No similarity: "cat" and "dog" are as far apart as "cat" and "the". Every token is equally distant.
  • Order-blind: summing one-hots into a bag of words throws away word order entirely.
The fix, nextReplace the giant sparse vector with a short, dense, learned one: an embedding.

Tokens to numbersToken, then id, then embedding

The embeddingThe id is just a name. The vector is what carries meaning: dense (a few hundred numbers, not 50,000), and learned during training, exactly like the weights in Module 2 (ML and DL essentials).

Tokens to numbersAn embedding is a lookup table

The embedding layer is one big matrix: one row per token in the vocabulary, each row a vector of the same length. The token id just picks a row.

  • Shape: vocabulary size by embedding dimension, for example 50,000 by 300.
  • Token id 3184 returns row 3184, no math, just a lookup.
  • Every number in the matrix is learned, like any weight from class 2.4 (PyTorch fundamentals). In PyTorch it is nn.Embedding.

Meaning as a vectorword2vec: the ancestor idea

The old breakthrough: train the vectors so that similar words land near each other, and directions carry meaning.

$$ \text{king} - \text{man} + \text{woman} \approx \text{queen} $$

Just the ancestorword2vec gives one fixed vector per word. Modern LLMs learn richer, context-aware vectors, so "bank" differs in "river bank" and "bank loan". The payoff returns in class 4.4 (Embeddings and vector databases).

Meaning as a vectorHow word2vec learns those vectors

Slide a small window across the text and play a prediction game. There are two mirror-image versions, and no labels are needed: the text is its own training signal.

CBOW: context predicts the blank
Skip-gram: the word predicts its context
Which to picture, and why it worksCBOW is the fill-in-the-blank version, usually the easier intuition; skip-gram is the reverse and does better on rare words. Either way, a word is known by the company it keeps: "coffee" and "tea" share neighbors like "cup" and "drink", so they land near each other. Repeat across millions of windows and the vectors settle.

Meaning as a vectorThe shallow network behind it

word2vec is a tiny neural network, exactly the kind from Module 2 (ML and DL essentials). That is where the embeddings actually live.

  • Input: the one-hot vector of a word.
  • Hidden: a single linear layer, the weighted sum from class 2.3 (Neural networks), with no activation.
  • Output: softmax over the whole vocabulary, predicting a context word.
  • Trained by the same loop as class 2.4 (PyTorch fundamentals).
Where the embedding isBecause the input is one-hot, the hidden vector is simply one row of the input matrix \( W \). That matrix is the embedding table: after training we keep it and throw the output layer away.

Meaning as a vectorEmbeddings, and what runs on top of them

A common mix-up: do sequence models like RNNs learn the embeddings? Keep the two pieces separate. The embedding is a lookup table of vectors; the model is what processes the sequence of those vectors.

The distinctionThe embedding table is learned either by a dedicated algorithm like word2vec or GloVe, then reused, or jointly with the model as its first layer (nn.Embedding, trained by backprop). The RNN, LSTM, or transformer is a separate piece that consumes those vectors. It is not itself an embedding algorithm.

The needMeet RNNs and LSTMs

Before transformers, the standard sequence model was the recurrent neural network. It reads the embedding vectors one at a time, keeping a hidden state: a running memory it updates at each step and passes to the next.

  • Step by step: take the next word, combine it with the memory, produce a new memory.
  • An LSTM is an RNN with gates that decide what to keep, forget, and output, so it holds context longer.
Keep it lightYou do not need the internals of RNNs or LSTMs. Hold on to one idea: a memory passed forward from word to word. That alone explains their two weaknesses, a memory that fades over distance and a read that cannot be parallelized, which are exactly what attention fixes in class 3.2 (Attention).

The needAn RNN classifying a sentence

A classic use, before transformers: read a whole sentence, then decide a label. The RNN consumes the word vectors one at a time; its final hidden state is a summary of the sentence, which a small classifier turns into a prediction.

  • Sentiment: is this review positive or negative?
  • The last hidden state feeds a linear layer, then softmax (class 2.3, Neural networks).
  • The classifier reads only the final state, which has folded in all the earlier words. (Some models instead pool over every state.)

The needHow older models read, and forget

RNNs and LSTMs read one word at a time, carrying a running memory forward. The catch: that memory of early words fades as the sentence goes on, so distant context is lost.

The needSequential and slow, versus all at once

Reading one step at a time has a second cost: each word waits for the one before, so the work cannot be spread across a GPU.

What we needA mechanism where every word looks directly at every other word, at once: no fading memory, and fully parallel. That mechanism is attention, and it is the whole of class 3.2 (Attention).
