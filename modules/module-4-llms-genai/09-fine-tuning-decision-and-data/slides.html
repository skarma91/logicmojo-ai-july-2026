The decisionShould you even fine-tune? Usually no

Fine-tuning is expensive to do well: you need good data, a training run, evaluation, and a way to serve the result. Most problems are solved more cheaply by a better prompt or by retrieval. Reach for fine-tuning last, not first.

The order to try thingsStart with a better prompt. If the gap is missing knowledge, add RAG (classes 4.5 and 4.6). Only if the model still will not follow the behavior you need, fine-tune. Each step is cheaper and faster to change than the next.

They combineThese are not exclusive. A common production shape is a fine-tuned model for consistent behavior, plus RAG for fresh knowledge, plus a good prompt. Pick the cheapest lever that closes your specific gap.

The core ideaFine-tune for behavior, not facts

Fine-tuning shapes how the model responds: its style, format, and consistency. It is a poor way to add what it knows. Facts are better handled by RAG, which you can update instantly and cite.

Misconception"Fine-tune it on my documents so it knows them." It will not reliably learn facts that way, and it cannot cite them or stay current. Use RAG for knowledge; fine-tune to change behavior.

The mechanismWhat supervised fine-tuning does

Supervised fine-tuning (SFT) is the training loop from class 2.2 (How models learn), applied to an already-pretrained model on your own input-output examples. The model keeps predicting the next token; you just continue training so it adopts the pattern in your examples.

Same loop as class 2.2Forward pass, compute the loss against the target output, backpropagate, update. Nothing new in the mechanics; what changed is the starting point (a capable model) and the data (a few hundred of your examples).
What "learning a behavior" meansShow the model many examples of the answer style you want, and it shifts its weights toward producing that style by default, without you having to spell it out in every prompt. We call that target style the model's house style: borrowed from publishing, where a "house style" is the fixed set of rules an outlet applies to everything it prints (tone, formatting, how sources are cited). Here it means our assistant's rules: concise, always cite the publication and year, and decline politely when the answer is not covered.

Two axesSFT vs full vs PEFT: two different choices

"Supervised fine-tuning" and "full fine-tuning" are not the same thing, they answer different questions. Do not conflate them.

The choice
The options
What you train on (the objective)
SFT: learn from labeled input-output pairs. Or preference optimization (DPO, RLHF): learn from comparisons of better vs worse answers (class 4.10).
How much you update (the parameters)
Full fine-tuning: update every weight. Or PEFT: update a small subset, keep the base frozen.
They are independentYou can do SFT with full fine-tuning, or SFT with PEFT. Our build is SFT + QLoRA: the objective is supervised pairs, and the parameters are updated the PEFT way. Full fine-tuning here would retrain all of the weights, far too costly.
The PEFT familyPEFT = parameter-efficient fine-tuning. The common one is LoRA (and QLoRA), a small low-rank add-on. Others: adapters (tiny layers inserted between blocks), prefix and prompt tuning (train soft prompt vectors, weights untouched), and IA3 (learned scalings). We use LoRA, next.

Efficient fine-tuningLoRA: train a small add-on, freeze the rest

Updating every weight of a large model (full fine-tuning) needs a lot of memory and compute. LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation) freezes the original weights and trains a small add-on instead, so you update a tiny fraction of the parameters.

Why it worksThe change a task needs is usually low-rank: it can be captured by two small matrices rather than a full-size one. You learn those small matrices and leave the big model untouched, so you can keep many cheap add-ons for one base model.

The formulaThe low-rank update, decoded

LoRA replaces the fine-tuned weight with the frozen original plus a small learned correction:

$$ W' = W + B A $$

Symbol
In plain words
\( W \)
the original weight matrix, shape \(d \times d\). Frozen, never updated.
\( B, A \)
two small trained matrices, \(B\) is \(d \times r\) and \(A\) is \(r \times d\), with the rank \(r\) tiny (say 8 or 16).
\( B A \)
their product is \(d \times d\), the same shape as \(W\), a compact approximation of the change the task needs.
\( W' \)
the effective weight used at inference: original plus correction.
Why it is cheapA full update trains \(d \times d\) numbers; LoRA trains only \(2 d r\). For \(d = 4096\) and \(r = 8\) that is about \(65{,}000\) versus \(17\) million per matrix, a few hundred times fewer.

The intuitionWhy so few parameters can steer the whole model

It seems surprising that training well under one percent of the weights changes how the model behaves. Three reasons make it work.

The skill is already therePretraining (class 3.4, pretrained transformers) already gave the model broad ability. A behavior tune does not teach it to write from scratch; it re-weights capability it already has, so a small nudge is enough.
The change is low-rankThe weight update a single task needs lives in a tiny subspace (its intrinsic rank is small). A rank-8 or rank-16 correction \(BA\) captures most of it, so two thin matrices do the work of a full one.
The base stays intactBecause \(W\) is frozen, the general knowledge in it cannot be overwritten. You steer behavior with the add-on while every original skill is preserved by construction.
Catastrophic forgettingWhen you retrain every weight (full fine-tuning) on a narrow dataset, the model can overwrite unrelated skills it learned in pretraining and get worse at everything else. This is catastrophic forgetting. Freezing \(W\) and training only \(BA\) largely sidesteps it, though you still watch for it: a small or repetitive set can make even a LoRA tune overfit and drift, so in class 4.10 (Fine-tuning II) we check that general answers did not degrade.

Where the adapter goesWhich weights get an adapter (not all of them)

A transformer has many weight matrices per layer. LoRA does not adapt all of them; you choose which ones (the target_modules setting). Every matrix you pick gets its own pair \(B, A\); the rest stay frozen.

The usual choiceThe four attention projections: query, key, value, and output (q_proj, k_proj, v_proj, o_proj). This is our notebook's default and a strong, cheap baseline that steers most behavior.
More capacityAlso adapt the feed-forward (MLP) projections (gate, up, down). This reaches more of the network for harder behavior shifts, at the cost of a larger adapter and more overfit risk.
Usually left aloneToken embeddings, the final output head, and layer-norms are normally not adapted; touching them adds many parameters for little behavior gain.
The knobs togetherTwo dials set the adapter size: the rank \(r\) (how big each \(BA\) is) and how many modules you target. More of either means more it can learn, a bigger adapter, and more chance of overfitting a small set.

On free hardwareQLoRA: LoRA on a 4-bit base

QLoRA combines LoRA with quantization (class 4.7): store the frozen base model in 4-bit so it barely uses memory, and train the small LoRA add-on on top of it in 16-bit. The heavy part shrinks, the trained part stays accurate.

How the two precisions work together4-bit is only how the base is stored. On the forward pass each 4-bit weight is dequantized to 16-bit just for that matmul, then discarded, so the activations are full precision. The LoRA matrices \(B, A\) stay in 16-bit throughout. On backprop, gradients flow through the math but update only \(B, A\); the frozen base is never written back, so it stays 4-bit. In short: 4-bit for storage, 16-bit for the arithmetic and for the part that actually learns.
Why it mattersA base that needed a large GPU at 16-bit fits the free Colab T4 (16 GB VRAM) at 4-bit, so fine-tuning stops requiring expensive hardware. Our 0.5B model is tiny even for a T4; QLoRA is what makes far larger bases trainable there. This is the setup we prepare today and run in class 4.10 (Fine-tuning II).

By exampleDequantize and discard: one row of weights

Take one neuron with four weights. Watch them go from 16-bit, to 4-bit storage, back to 16-bit only for the multiply.

Stage
What is held
True weights (bf16)
[ 0.42, -0.13, 0.05, -0.37 ], at 16 bits each is 64 bits total
Stored in VRAM (4-bit)
codes [ 15, 6, 8, 4 ] (4 bits each) + one shared scale = 0.42 per block. This is all that sits in memory.
Rebuilt for the matmul (bf16)
table[code] × scale = [ 0.42, -0.13, 0.01, -0.37 ], used in W · x, then discarded.
Just in time, then goneThe 16-bit values are reconstructed in fast on-chip memory the instant they are needed, used for that one W · x, and never written back. Next time, they are rebuilt again from the same codes. Memory only ever holds the 4-bit form.
Lossy on purposeNotice 0.05 came back as 0.01: the base is permanently a little wrong. That is fine, because the frozen base only needs to be roughly right, and the LoRA add-on that actually learns stays full 16-bit.
Mental model4-bit is how the weight is filed away; 16-bit is the photocopy the GPU makes to do one calculation, then shreds.

What you can actually tuneFine-tuning is mostly an open-weight move

Everything today assumes you can see the weights. That is why LoRA and QLoRA live on open-weight models (Llama, Qwen, Mistral, Gemma): you download the base, train an adapter, and host the result yourself.

Open-weight: yours to tuneWeights are published, so you own the whole loop, train on your hardware or a free Colab GPU, keep the adapter, and serve it wherever you like. No per-token premium, no dependence on a vendor keeping the feature alive.
Frontier closed models: mostly not, or costlyGPT, Claude, and Gemini do not release weights, so you cannot LoRA them yourself. A subset offer a managed fine-tune (your data uploaded to their platform, SFT only), but it is constrained and pricey: the tuned model is billed at a premium per token, and some require dedicated (provisioned) capacity billed by the hour whether or not you use it. Availability shifts, too (one major provider began winding its fine-tuning API down in 2026).
TakeawayDefault to an open-weight base when you fine-tune. For a closed frontier model, prefer a better prompt and RAG; reach for its managed tuning only when the behavior truly cannot be gotten any other way and the ongoing cost is justified.

The dataPrepare and format the examples

Fine-tuning is only as good as its examples. Prepare clean input-output pairs in the chat format the trainer expects, each a short conversation ending in the answer you want the model to imitate.

Quality over quantity, but enough of itA few hundred clean, consistent examples beat thousands of noisy ones; every example should show the exact behavior you want, since one sloppy target teaches the wrong habit. Our shipped set is small and paraphrase-augmented for teaching; a real behavior tune still wants a few hundred to about a thousand clean examples.
The chat formatEach row is a list of messages: a system rule, the user question, and the assistant target answer. The trainer applies the model's chat template so training matches how the model is prompted at inference.

A rule you must not breakKeep training and evaluation separate

Split your examples into a training set (what the model learns from) and a small validation set (what you watch during training). And never train on your eval set from class 4.8 (Evaluating LLM and RAG): that is your honest, held-out test.

WhyA model scored on data it trained on looks great and generalizes poorly (overfitting, from class 2.1). The 4.8 eval set stays untouched so it can still tell you, in 4.10, whether the fine-tune actually helped.
