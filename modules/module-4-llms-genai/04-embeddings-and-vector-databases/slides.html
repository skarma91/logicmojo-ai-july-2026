The problemGive the model memory of your documents

An LLM knows what it read during training. It does not know your company handbook, your product docs, or last week's tickets. Asking it about them gives a confident guess, not an answer.

The fix is retrieval: keep your documents in a searchable store, find the passages relevant to a question, and hand them to the model. The missing piece is a way to search by meaning, also called semantic search, rather than by exact words.

EmbeddingsMeaning becomes a point in space

An embedding is a vector that captures the meaning of a piece of text. Texts that mean similar things land near each other; unrelated texts land far apart. Semantic search becomes geometry: find the nearest points to the question.

Why this is powerfulThe query "download my bills" shares no words with "export invoices to CSV", yet they sit close because they mean the same thing. Keyword search would miss it; semantic search finds it.

SimilarityCosine similarity, decoded

To measure how close two meanings are, measure the angle between their vectors. A small angle means they point the same way, so they mean nearly the same thing.

$$ \cos(a, b) \;=\; \frac{a \cdot b}{\lVert a \rVert \; \lVert b \rVert} $$

MisconceptionIt stores meaning, not the words

"An embedding stores the text." It does not. It stores a list of numbers, the coordinates of that text in meaning space. Think of it like a map pin: the pin's coordinates tell you where something is and what sits near it, but the coordinates are not the place itself.

ConsequenceYou cannot read a document back out of its embedding, just as you cannot read a street address out of two map coordinates. The vector is for finding; the original text is stored separately, for reading and for feeding to the model.
How an embedding is madeA transformer-based encoder (class 3.3, The Transformer) reads the passage and pools its per-token outputs into one vector. The all-MiniLM model we use averages them (mean pooling); some models instead use the special [CLS] token. Averaging only works because the input is short: these models cap the length (a few hundred tokens) and truncate the rest, and averaging many tokens would blur the meaning. That is exactly why you embed short chunks, not whole documents (next slide).

ChunkingSplit long documents into passages

You do not embed a whole 40-page handbook as one vector; its meaning would be an average mush. You split it into chunks, small passages, and embed each. Neighboring chunks share a small overlap: the same few sentences appear at the end of one chunk and the start of the next, so a sentence sitting on the boundary stays whole in at least one chunk instead of being cut in half and losing its meaning.

The trade-offChunks too small lose context; too large dilute the meaning and cost more tokens. A few hundred words with a small overlap is a common starting point, then tune.

Vector databaseStore the vectors, find the nearest fast

A vector database stores your embeddings and, given a query vector, returns the nearest ones. The naive way compares the query against every stored vector, one by one. That is fine for thousands, far too slow for millions.

So it builds an index: it groups nearby vectors ahead of time (for example into clusters, or a graph of "who is close to whom"). At query time it visits only the few groups near the query and skips the rest, so it checks a small fraction of the data instead of all of it. This is approximate nearest-neighbor search: a little accuracy traded for a large speed gain.

The common indexHNSW: the graph that makes search fast

The "graph of who is close to whom" has a name: HNSW (Hierarchical Navigable Small World), the index most vector databases use by default.

Build: link each vector to its nearest neighbors to form a graph, then stack it in layers. The top layer has few nodes with long-range links (express routes); each lower layer adds more nodes and shorter, local links.

Search: enter at the top, hop to the neighbor closest to the query, drop a layer, and repeat. You touch a handful of nodes, not all N. Like a road atlas: country, then region, then street.

The trade-off and where it livesApproximate: it can miss the true nearest now and then, but recall is very high in practice, tuned by how wide you search. Our class uses flat, exact IndexFlatIP because the corpus is tiny; at scale, FAISS, Chroma, Qdrant, and pgvector default to HNSW.

So "managed" is about who runs it, not a different algorithm.

Metadata filteringCombine meaning with hard filters

Store fields alongside each vector: source, year, section, author. A filtered search then means "the closest passages by meaning, but only those whose fields match this rule."

Worked exampleQuery: "what is our refund policy?" over a corpus where the same wording appears in two documents: {section: "Refunds", year: 2024} and an old {section: "Refunds", year: 2019}. Both are near the query by meaning, so plain semantic search may return the 2019 one. Add the filter year = 2024 and the search keeps only current passages, so the model never even sees the outdated policy. The same pattern restricts a search to one product, one customer, or one team.

Two kinds of searchMeaning search vs keyword search

Semantic (meaning) search

Matches by embedding closeness, so it catches paraphrases, the same idea in different words. "download my bills" finds "export invoices". But it can miss an exact string it never learned to associate.

Keyword search (BM25)

Matches by exact word overlap, scoring rarer shared words higher. It nails a product code, an error number, or a proper name, but misses a paraphrase that shares no words.

Paraphrase, definedTwo texts that carry the same meaning with different words ("cancel my plan" and "end my subscription"). Meaning search treats them as close; keyword search does not, because they share no words.

Hybrid searchRun both, then blend the scores

Hybrid search runs the meaning search and the keyword search on the same query, normalizes each set of scores to a common scale, and adds them with a weight: \( \text{score} = \alpha \cdot \text{semantic} + (1-\alpha) \cdot \text{keyword} \). Here \( \alpha \) is a fraction between 0 and 1: \( \alpha = 1 \) is pure meaning search, \( \alpha = 0 \) is pure keyword search, and values in between blend the two. You get paraphrase matching and exact-string matching at once.

Another way to blend: RRFBlending raw scores needs normalization. A common alternative, Reciprocal Rank Fusion (RRF), merges the two ranked lists by rank position instead of raw scores, so no normalization is needed. Same goal, a different recipe; we use the weighted-score blend here for its simplicity.

Top-kHow many passages to fetch

Top-k is how many passages the retriever returns. It is a real trade-off: too few and you miss the passage that held the answer; too many and you bury the model in noise and pay for extra tokens.

A sensible defaultStart around \( k = 3 \) to \( 5 \) for short docs, then adjust by watching whether answers miss context or drift off into irrelevant passages.
