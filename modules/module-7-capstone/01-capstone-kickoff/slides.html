The business problemA support inbox full of the same questions

An online shop gets thousands of support messages a week. Most ask the same few things: cancel my order, where is my refund, reset my password. A few are angry customers, payment failures, or people asking for a human.

Customers waitA routine question sits in the same queue as a serious one. "How do I reset my password" waits hours for a two-line answer.
Agents burn outSkilled people spend their day pasting the same help-centre steps, and have less time for the hard cases that need judgment.
The business paysSupport cost grows in step with sales: twice the orders means roughly twice the tickets and twice the staff.

The goal: answer routine tickets at once with a correct, grounded reply, and send everything risky to a person, with a summary ready.

The ideaUse a model for the two language jobs (understanding the ticket, writing the reply). Use plain code for every decision that must be predictable: who gets a human, and what a reply may say.

The dataReal, public, labeled: the Bitext support dataset

What it is
  • 26,872 messages, each with an example agent reply.
  • 27 intents in 11 categories.
  • Tags for colloquial text, typos, and offensive language (W).
  • License CDLA-Sharing-1.0.
How we use it
  • A committed sample: 174 real rows, all 27 intents.
  • 27 help articles: the most detailed reply per intent.
  • 147 labeled tickets: every other row, never an article.
  • A fetch script rebuilds both from the full CSV.
Said plainlyBitext calls the data "hybrid synthetic": written in the style of real tickets, not copied from customers. Safe to share; real tickets will be messier.

The approachWhy a small team of agents, not one

Triage (model)One narrow job: name the intent, urgency, sentiment, and how sure it is. A short prompt with a closed list of 27 intents.
Knowledge (no model)Find the help article by retrieval. A second, independent opinion that costs nothing and cannot invent anything.
Reply writer (model)Draft a reply from one article only. It never decides whether to reply; it only writes.

The split follows the senior default from class 6.1 (Multi-agent architectures): split only on a named reason. Here there are two: each job gets the smallest possible context, and the safety decisions move out of any prompt into code we can test.

Misconception"More agents means a smarter system"

The tempting design
  • A planner agent, a sentiment agent, a policy agent, a tone agent, a reviewer agent, a manager agent.
  • Every hop is another model call, more latency, more cost, and another place to fail.
  • Five steps at 95% each succeed together only 77% of the time.
Our design
  • Three agents, and one of them is not even a model.
  • The supervisor is about 50 lines of plain code.
  • A routine ticket costs two model calls; an escalation can cost one.

The strongest design is the simplest one that meets the requirements. Add an agent only when you can name what it fixes.

The judgmentA framework earns its place with long-running, resumable, or branching workflows. One ticket in, one decision out, does not need it. Moving this design onto LangGraph later is a small, mechanical change, because the agents do not know who calls them.

The approachSix principles the design follows

1. Redact firstPersonal data becomes placeholders before any model, log, or trace sees the ticket.
2. Rules in codeEscalation and reply checks are Python functions, not prompt instructions a model may ignore.
3. Never trust model JSONEvery field the model returns is checked, with a safe fallback.
4. A second opinionRetrieval checks triage. When they disagree on an unsure ticket, a person decides.
5. Bounded everythingAt most two drafts, at most eight steps. Then a person takes over.
6. Measure itEvery step is traced, and an evaluation on labeled tickets decides when it is good enough.

High-level designThe architecture on one page

Component 1The redaction gate

What it does
  • Finds emails, phone numbers, card numbers, ID numbers, and names after a cue such as "I'm" or "my name is".
  • Replaces each with a typed placeholder: <EMAIL_1>, <PERSON_1>.
  • Keeps the originals in a vault, in memory only.
  • Puts them back only in the final reply to the customer who wrote the ticket.
Example
in : I'm Ana Ruiz, ana@example.com.
     How do I cancel order 4812?
out: I'm <PERSON_1>, <EMAIL_1>.
     How do I cancel order 4812?
Why firstThe order number stays: it is not personal data, and the agents need it. Everything downstream, models, traces, and handoff notes, sees only placeholders. This is the redaction module from class 6.4 (Guardrails, routing, and compliance).

Component 2The triage agent

Contract
  • In: the redacted ticket text.
  • Out: a Triage: intent (one of 27, or unknown), urgency, sentiment, confidence.
  • How: one model call answering in JSON, with the 27 intents in the prompt.
  • Cost: one call, every ticket.
Never trust the JSON
  • An intent outside the list becomes unknown: a person takes it.
  • Missing urgency becomes normal; missing sentiment, calm.
  • An unreadable confidence becomes 0, which escalates.
  • No JSON at all escalates. The desk fails toward "ask a person".
Structured outputThe same idea as class 4.3 (Structured outputs and tool calling): ask for a shape, then validate it in code.

Component 3The knowledge agent: retrieval as a second opinion

Contract
  • In: the redacted ticket and triage's intent.
  • Out: an Article: the help article for that intent, a similarity score, and agrees: was it also retrieval's own top match?
  • How: TF-IDF vectors over the 27 articles, compared by cosine similarity. No model, no cost.
TF-IDF in one line

$$w_{t,d} = \mathrm{tf}_{t,d} \times \log\frac{N}{\mathrm{df}_t}$$

\(w_{t,d}\) is the weight of term \(t\) in article \(d\); \(\mathrm{tf}_{t,d}\) is how often \(t\) appears in \(d\); \(N\) is the number of articles (27); \(\mathrm{df}_t\) is how many articles contain \(t\). A term frequent in one article but rare overall scores high; a word in every article scores near zero.

Terms are letter piecesTickets are full of typos ("oorder", "acconut"), so the terms are 3 to 5 letter chunks inside words, not whole words. Measured on the 147 labeled tickets: the right article ranked first 69% of the time, against 50% with whole words.

Component 4The reply writer agent

Contract
  • In: the redacted ticket, one Article, and, on a retry, the list of problems with the last draft.
  • Out: a Draft: the reply text.
  • How: one model call with a strict prompt: under 120 words, only what the article says, keep every placeholder.
  • Cost: one call per draft; at most two drafts.
What it is not allowed to do
  • Decide whether to reply. The supervisor already did.
  • Check its own work. The guards do, in code.
  • See other articles, the customer's history, or any real personal data.
Less context, fewer inventionsA writer that sees one article cannot quote the wrong policy from another. Scoped context is the class 6.3 (Context engineering) habit.

Component 5The supervisor: a loop that reads the state

The loop
  1. Redact the ticket and create a CaseState.
  2. Ask next_step(state) for a step name.
  3. Run that step; it fills one field of the state.
  4. Repeat until the step is send or escalate.
  5. After 8 steps, stop and escalate, whatever happened.
Why rules, not a model, route
  • Routing here is a handful of if-statements; a model adds cost and randomness for nothing.
  • next_step() never calls a model, so routing is tested without spending a token.
  • Every decision is explainable: the trail of steps and the escalation reason.
ContrastIn class 6.1 (Multi-agent architectures) a model supervisor chose the next worker, because the path depended on open-ended research. A support ticket's path is known in advance, so code is the better supervisor.

Component 6The guards: the policy, in one file

escalation_reason(state)

Returns why a person must take over now, or nothing. Checked before every step: unclear intent, company policy, an angry or urgent customer, an unsure triage, a weak or disputed article, or two failed drafts.

check_reply(reply, article, ticket)

Returns the problems that make a draft unsafe: empty, too long, numbers or placeholders that appear in neither the article nor the ticket, or personal data the writer could not have known.

Why one fileA product manager or a compliance reviewer can read the whole policy here without opening a single prompt. Every threshold is a named constant at the top of the file, set from data and easy to change.

Component 7Tracing and evaluation

The tracer
  • One JSON line per step: name, ticket id, milliseconds, model calls, tokens, error.
  • Model calls are counted from the usage log in llm.py, so the agents need no changes.
  • No ticket text in a span: a trace file can be shared without leaking personal data.
  • The full version, nested trees and LangSmith, is class 6.6 (Observability and tracing).
The evaluation
  • Runs the desk on the 147 labeled tickets.
  • Reports the success metrics from the start of today, with a 95% Wilson interval on intent accuracy.
  • A retrieval-only mode scores the knowledge agent with no model at all.
  • Same habits as class 6.7 (Evaluating agentic systems): define success first, then measure it.

Low-level designThe three agent classes share one shape

class TriageAgent:                  # name = "triage"
    def run(self, text) -> Triage                 # one model call, JSON validated

class KnowledgeAgent:               # name = "knowledge"; fits TF-IDF once
    def scores(self, text)                        # cosine similarity to all 27
    def run(self, text, intent) -> Article        # no model call

class ReplyWriterAgent:             # name = "writer"
    def run(self, text, article, feedback=None) -> Draft   # one call per draft
The conventionEach agent has a name, used to log its model calls, and one run() that returns one dataclass. Agents never see CaseState, so each is testable alone.

Every failure path ends in the same place: a person, with a reason. The desk is allowed to be unhelpful; it is not allowed to be wrong.

CostWhat one ticket costs

$$\text{calls per ticket} = \underbrace{1}_{\text{triage}} + \underbrace{d}_{\text{drafts}}, \qquad d \in \{0, 1, 2\}$$

Early escalation\(d = 0\): one call. Angry, urgent, policy, and unclear tickets stop after triage.
Routine reply\(d = 1\): two calls. The common path.
Rejected draft\(d = 2\): three calls, then a reply or a person.
On a free tier (illustrative)At about two calls per ticket and Groq's free limit of roughly 30 requests a minute for openai/gpt-oss-20b, one key handles about 15 tickets a minute. Retrieval, routing, and checks are free. Real per-agent numbers come from the trace.

EvaluationHow we will know it works

The ground truth
  • 147 labeled tickets, never used as help articles.
  • Needs a person when the intent is in ALWAYS_HUMAN, or Bitext tagged the message as offensive (W).
  • Intent labels come with the dataset.
Measured already, no model

Retrieval alone ranks the right article first for 102 of 147 tickets (69%) and in the top three for 129 (88%). Good enough to check triage, not to replace it.

Read the numbers in this order
  1. Escalation recall: did every risky ticket reach a person?
  2. Escalation precision: how much human time was wasted?
  3. Auto-resolved rate: how much work did the desk take off the team?
  4. Intent accuracy, with its 95% Wilson interval: 147 tickets is a small sample.
  5. Calls and tokens per ticket.

LimitsPrivacy, and what is out of scope

Privacy by design
  • Redaction before any model call; restore only in the final reply.
  • Traces hold step names and numbers, never ticket text.
  • Handoff summaries use the redacted text.
  • Synthetic data: no real customer data in the repository.
Out of scope, on purpose
  • Acting on real accounts: the desk explains steps.
  • Multi-turn chat: one ticket in, one outcome out.
  • Languages other than English.
  • Learning from feedback: people make the changes.
Scope is a featureA finished, measured desk beats a half-built one that promises everything. Each item on the right is a clean next step.
