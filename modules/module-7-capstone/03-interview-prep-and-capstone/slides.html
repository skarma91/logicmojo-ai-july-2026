The business problemPlanning a trip takes hours, and the shortcuts lie

A traveller has four days in Lisbon, likes food and history, and wants a relaxed pace. Today they read a dozen pages, open a map, and assemble a plan by hand over an evening or two.

Search and guidebooksAccurate, but the traveller does all the assembly: which places, which day, in what order.
A general chatbotFast and fluent, but it invents attractions, ignores the number of days, and packs eight sights into a "relaxed" day.
A travel agentGood plans, but slow and expensive for a short city break.

The goal: a day-by-day plan in under a minute that respects the traveller's constraints, uses only places found in a real guide, and cites where each one came from.

The business problemWhat a good plan must satisfy

Hard constraints: checkable in code
  • Days: exactly as many days as requested.
  • Pace: no more than a set number of activities per day for the chosen pace.
  • Grounding: every place appears in the guide text the system retrieved, with its source.
  • Interests: each stated interest shows up somewhere in the plan.
  • No repeats: the same place is not scheduled twice.
Soft qualities: judged, not counted
  • Days that make geographic sense.
  • Variety across the trip.
  • Short notes a traveller would find useful.
The design consequenceHard constraints go to code, where they are cheap and never ignored. Only the soft qualities need a model's judgment.

The business problemWhat success looks like, in numbers

Metric
Plain meaning
Constraint pass rate
The share of plans that meet every hard constraint, with a 95% Wilson interval
Grounding rate
The share of scheduled places found in the retrieved guide text. Anything below 100% is a hallucination to explain.
Interest coverage
The share of requested interests that appear in the plan
Revisions per request
How often the critique sends a plan back; high means the planner is weak or the checks are too strict
Correct refusals
For a destination with no guide, the share of runs that say so instead of inventing a plan
Cost and latency
Model calls, tokens, and seconds per request, from your traces

Grounding comes first. A short plan of real places beats a long plan with one invented museum.

The dataWikivoyage: a free, worldwide travel guide

What it is
  • An open travel guide written by volunteers, covering cities worldwide.
  • Each city page follows the same sections: Understand, Get around, See, Do, Eat, Drink, Stay safe.
  • Licensed CC BY-SA 4.0: reuse it with credit, and share what you build from it under the same terms.
What you get
  • A fetch script that pulls guides through the public API, one request a second.
  • Short chunks, one paragraph or listing each, labelled with destination, section, and page URL.
  • A committed sample covering 12 cities on five continents.
Why this dataSections tell you what a chunk is about: a "See" chunk is a sight, an "Eat" chunk is a place to eat. That structure is free signal for your retrieval and your planner.

The approachPlan, critique, revise: why a team fits

ResearcherFinds guide text that matches the destination and the traveller's interests. Its output is the only pool of places the plan may use.
PlannerTurns that pool into a day-by-day plan for the requested days and pace. The only agent that writes the itinerary.
CriticChecks the plan: hard constraints in code first, then a model's judgment on the soft qualities. Sends specific feedback back to the planner.

This is the writer and reviewer loop from class 6.2 (Orchestration). A first plan is rarely perfect; a bounded critique and revision is cheaper than one giant prompt that tries to get everything right at once.

Misconception"The model already knows Lisbon. Skip retrieval."

What goes wrong without it
  • Plausible places that do not exist, or closed years ago.
  • No source, so nobody can check a claim.
  • Smaller and local models know far less, so quality swings with the model you can afford.
What retrieval gives you
  • A closed pool of candidate places with page URLs.
  • A grounding check that is a simple membership test in code.
  • The same behaviour on a small free model as on a large one.

The model is a planner, not an encyclopedia. Everything it schedules must come from the retrieved text.

The approachDesign principles to carry over

Redact firstTravellers mention names, emails, and phone numbers. None of it reaches a model or a trace.
Rules in codeDay count, pace, grounding, and repeats are checked by functions, not by asking a model nicely.
Never trust model JSONValidate every field of every structured reply, with a safe fallback.
Bounded loopsA fixed maximum number of revisions, then return the best valid plan or an honest failure.
Refuse clearlyNo guide for the destination, or impossible constraints, means saying so, not inventing.
Measure itTrace every step and evaluate on a fixed set of requests before you claim it works.

High-level designThe architecture on one page

Component 1Intake: redact, then read the constraints

Its job
  • Remove personal data from the request before anything else sees it.
  • Turn a free-text request into explicit constraints: destination, number of days, pace, interests.
  • Reject what it cannot plan: no destination, zero days, or a destination with no guide.
Example

Your decisionRead the constraints with a model and validate them in code, or offer a structured form and skip the model here. Both are defensible; say which and why.

Component 2The researcher: build the pool of real places

Its job
  • Find the guide chunks for the destination that match each interest and each useful section.
  • Return a pool of candidate places, each with its section and page URL.
  • Report when the guide is too thin to plan the requested days.
Questions your design must answer
  • How do you search: TF-IDF, character n-grams, or embeddings? Measure before you choose.
  • How do you turn a paragraph into a named place the critic can check?
  • How big a pool for a four-day trip, and how do you balance sections?
No model requiredRetrieval can be plain code, as the support desk's knowledge agent was. Spend model calls where language judgment is needed.

Component 3The planner: a plan from the pool, and only the pool

Its job
  • Arrange places from the pool into the requested days at the requested pace.
  • Return a structured plan your code can check, not prose.
  • On a revision, fix exactly what the critic reported.
What it must not do
  • Add a place that is not in the pool.
  • Invent opening hours, prices, or travel times the guide does not state.
  • See personal data or the whole guide; give it the pool and the constraints.
Structured outputAsk for a fixed shape and validate it, as in class 4.3 (Structured outputs and tool calling). A plan your code cannot parse counts as a failed attempt.

Component 4The critic: code first, judgment second

Exact checks, in code
  • The plan has exactly the requested number of days.
  • No day exceeds the pace limit.
  • Every place is in the researcher's pool.
  • Every interest appears at least once; no place appears twice.
Judgment, by a model
  • Do the days make sense as days? Variety, rhythm, rough geography.
  • Only asked once the exact checks pass, so you never pay for an opinion on a broken plan.
Feedback that works"Day 3 has 5 activities; the relaxed limit is 3" fixes a plan. "Please improve the plan" does not. The critic's output is the planner's next input, so make it specific.

Component 5The supervisor and the review loop

Its job
  • Run the researcher once, then the planner and the critic in a loop.
  • Stop when the critic approves, or after a fixed number of revisions.
  • On the cap: return the best plan that passes the exact checks, clearly marked, or refuse.
  • Hold a hard step limit, whatever the rules say.
How many rounds to expect

$$E[\text{rounds}] = \frac{1 - (1-q)^k}{q}$$

\(q\) is the chance a plan passes the critic, \(k\) the revision cap. With \(q = 0.6\) and \(k = 3\): \((1 - 0.4^3)/0.6 \approx 1.56\) rounds. This is the bounded-loop formula from class 6.2 (Orchestration); measure your own \(q\) from traces.

Your decisionPlain Python, like the support desk, or LangGraph, like Module 6 (Agentic AI). Either is fine; justify it in your design document.

Component 6Tracing and evaluation

Tracing
  • One record per step: which agent, how long, how many model calls and tokens, any error.
  • No request text or personal data in traces.
  • Enough to answer "why did request 7 take four rounds?" from the file alone.
Evaluation
  • At least 10 fixed requests across several cities, paces, and trip lengths.
  • Include hard cases: a city with no guide, more interests than days, a one-day trip.
  • Report every metric from the success slide, with intervals where n is small.
  • The methods from class 6.7 (Evaluating agentic systems).
